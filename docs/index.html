What would you learn if you had a tutor of your own? Name the skill you'd hate to lose, and ask for five minutes a day, or fifteen a week. HalfLife writes it, pitched at the level you choose, and forbids each issue from re-explaining what the one before it covered. It runs inside the AI tool you already have open — no API key, no account.

The idea it turns on

Depth changes what you are assumed to know — not the word count

Most "levels" are the same article at three lengths. Here, a level sets what may be skipped, so two pieces on one topic should be disjoint, not nested. Below are five pieces on one subject, each written to a different level. Same subject. Same five minutes.

Same five levels, same rubric, different subject — switch and stay on a level.

Openings from real eval runs. Long passages are cut, and every cut is marked with an ellipsis; nothing is reordered and no wording is changed. Every piece was generated fresh, judged blind against the rubric by a separate model, and scored correct at the level it was asked for. The full pieces are in the repository under evals/output/. The level descriptions above are shared between subjects, because the rubric does not know what it is being pointed at.

Why it accumulates

A series, not a pile of articles

Issue six knows what issue one established, and is forbidden from teaching it again. That is held in structured state rather than a prose summary, because a summary drifts from what was actually written.

The plan

An advisory arc drawn once, so the series does not random-walk. Each issue reports which entry it took — deviating is allowed, misreporting is not.

The coverage ledger

One short claim per line, appended by every issue, fed back into the next as ground that may be referred to but never re-explained.

Open threads

Things an issue deliberately deferred. The next one picks them up or drops them — no "more on this later" in the prose. You can open one yourself: tell the series it missed something, and the next issue is told your request outranks the plan.

Your feedback

Two axes, because they are different complaints. Too basic and too advanced move the depth. Already knew this and wrong subject leave the depth alone and move the ground — the level was right, the material was not.

Where it runs

Inside the harness you already have

HalfLife is an MCP server. Your own tool — Claude Code, Claude Cowork, anything that speaks MCP — writes the issues on the model it already runs. There is no API key, no subscription, and no service holding your reading history. 

$ halflife init                      # once per machine: creates the database
# then, in your harness, in plain language:

  "Subscribe me to giving feedback to a direct report, depth 3, five minutes, daily."
  "Plan the series."
  "Anything due? Write it."

A separate path calls the Anthropic API directly, for unattended delivery and for the evals — a pinned model is the only way output stays comparable between runs. Roughly $0.08 an issue, about $2.50 a month for a daily subscription. Every delivery records which path produced it.

Evidence

Measured, and the failures are on the record

The depth rubric is the highest-leverage text in the product, so it is versioned and graded. Six versions of it, each with its run recorded — including the ones that measured worse, and the revision whose own fix had to be checked for breaking the boundary next to it.

What is checkedResultHow
Was each piece written to the level asked for? 44 / 45 9 topics × 5 levels, judged blind against the rubric
Are two levels apart genuinely disjoint? 0 of 18 both pairs on every topic, including the harder adjacent one
Does a later issue re-teach an earlier one? 0 restatements 17 references across a six-issue series, judged at three ranges
Does the machinery hold together? 268 tests fully offline; anything judging output lives in evals
Does a level ever run out of ground? yes, at depth 1 one series judged 1, 1, 1, then 4 as its ledger grew — orientation is finite, so a depth-1 series now stops at three issues

What that does not prove. One judge model, nine topics, and a rubric tuned against them — further gains on that set may be fitting the judge rather than improving the writing. Reasoning effort moves the score more than two of the six revisions did. The numbers exist to say where to look; you are still the judge of whether your own issue was worth the time you gave it. None of them cover the classification half, which has one real run behind it and cannot be graded the way the writing was — a judge able to score it would need the conversation the design exists to keep private.

Status

Early, and specific about it

The generation engine and the harness integration are built and measured. The half that classifies what you worked on is built and not measured. Everything else is a deliberately later step.

  • Built: the depth rubric, series continuity with ledger compaction, the MCP server, the CLI, the eval harness that grades all of it, and session extraction — ask your harness to log what you worked on and it records subject names and one of seven behavioural verbs.
  • Built, and now checkable: the privacy boundary used to be a written constraint with no schema behind it. The signal table exists now, and the evidence column is refused a value by the write path — so “nothing populates it” is a test rather than a promise. The conversation is never passed to HalfLife at all: the recording function has no parameter that could carry it.
  • Not built: nothing reads those signals back. No aggregate surface beyond a count over a time window, no control plane, no dashboard — so everything the design says about org admins and aggregation describes what must hold when there is one, not what holds today. No server, no accounts, no multi-user, no deployment story. Single machine, single reader, SQLite.

Useful today if you want a daily read on something you are keeping alive. Not yet a product.
